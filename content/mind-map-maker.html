Choosing the right diagram

Mind map, flowchart or concept map?

DiagramShapeUse it forExample
Mind mapOne central idea with branchesRevising a whole topic, planning an essay, brainstorming“Causes of World War I”: alliances, militarism, imperialism, nationalism, the assassination
FlowchartBoxes joined by arrows in orderProcesses, cycles, algorithms, sequences of eventsThe water cycle, mitosis, how a bill becomes law
Concept mapIdeas joined by labelled linksShowing how ideas relate and depend on each otherPhotosynthesis linked to respiration, energy and food chains

Getting a better first draft

  • Be specific. “Photosynthesis for a year 9 class: inputs, outputs, where it happens, limiting factors” gives a far more useful map than “photosynthesis”.
  • Name the level. Mention the age group or exam so the vocabulary fits.
  • Ask for a size. Five to eight branches is easy to read on one page; more becomes cluttered.
  • Generate, then edit. Delete what you don't need, add your own examples, and colour-code the branches you find hardest.

Why editable matters

Many AI diagram tools produce a picture: you can look at it, but changing one label means generating the whole thing again. In Chalkmind every box and arrow is an ordinary shape on your board, just like the ones you draw by hand. You can drag a branch to make room, rename a step, delete one, or draw your own arrows between ideas. That's also what makes it useful for learning: rearranging a map yourself is a far better way to understand a topic than reading a finished one.

Ideas for the classroom

  • Build it together: start with a map of just the main branches and let the class suggest the details.
  • Spot the gap: generate a map, delete one branch, and ask students what's missing.
  • Before and after: make a map at the start of a unit and rebuild it at the end to see what students learned.
  • Revision lesson: one map per topic, exported as a PDF revision pack.
