Most lost marks in maths homework aren't from not knowing the topic. They're from small slips: a dropped minus sign, a bracket expanded halfway, a number copied wrongly from one line to the next. The good news is that these are the easiest mistakes to catch, if you check in the right way.

The key idea: don't check by rereading. Your brain sees what it expects to see. Each check below looks at your answer from a different angle, so a mistake has nowhere to hide.

1. Substitute the answer back in

For any equation, put your answer into the original equation (not one of your later lines) and see whether both sides match.

Example

You solved 4x − 7 = 2x + 9 and got x = 8.

Left side: 4(8) − 7 = 25. Right side: 2(8) + 9 = 25. They match, so x = 8 is right.

This works for simultaneous equations (check both equations), quadratics (check each solution) and inequalities (try a value just inside your answer range).

2. Estimate before you trust a number

Round the numbers and do a rough version in your head. If your exact answer is far from the estimate, something's wrong.

Example

48.7 × 21.3: about 50 × 20 = 1000. If your answer is 10 373 or 103.7, you've slipped a decimal place. (The exact answer is 1037.31.)

3. Check units and size

Ask whether the answer makes sense in the real world. A person who walks at 600 km/h, a triangle side of −4 cm, or a probability of 1.3 are all signs to look again. If a question has units, carry them through every line; mismatched units (metres and centimetres, minutes and hours) are a classic source of wrong answers.

4. Find the first line that doesn't follow

If your final answer fails a check, don't start again from scratch. Compare each line with the line before it and ask: does this line follow from the last one? The first line where the answer is “no” is your mistake. Everything after it can be right in method and still give a wrong answer.

Example
  1. 3(x − 2) = 2x + 5
  2. 3x − 2 = 2x + 5 ← doesn't follow: the 3 must multiply both terms
  3. x = 7

Fixing line 2 gives 3x − 6 = 2x + 5, so x = 11. Check: 3(9) = 27 and 2(11) + 5 = 27. ✓

5. Look for the five most common slips

Teachers see the same mistakes again and again. Scan your working for these specifically:

SlipWrongRight
Not distributing to every term2(x + 5) = 2x + 52x + 10
Minus sign before a bracket5 − (x − 3) = 5 − x − 35 − x + 3
Squaring a sum term by term(a + b)² = a² + b²a² + 2ab + b²
Dividing only one term(6x + 4) ÷ 2 = 3x + 43x + 2
Moving a term without changing its signx + 4 = 9 → x = 9 + 4x = 9 − 4

6. Work backwards, or use a different method

Solving the same way twice often repeats the same mistake. Instead:

  • Work backwards: start from your answer and undo each operation to see whether you get the question's numbers.
  • Use another method: check a factorised quadratic with the quadratic formula, or a calculated angle by checking the angles in the triangle add to 180°.
  • Draw it: a quick sketch of a graph or shape often shows an answer is impossible (a negative length, an obtuse angle in the wrong place).

7. Check what you copied

Compare the question in your book with the question on the sheet, and each number from one line to the next. Copying −3 as 3, or 56 as 65, is surprisingly common, and no amount of correct method fixes it.

A two-minute checking routine

  1. Reread the question: did you answer what was asked (units, rounding, form of the answer)?
  2. Substitute or estimate to test the final answer.
  3. If it fails, find the first line that doesn't follow from the one before.
  4. Scan for the five common slips.
  5. Check numbers you copied between lines.

Do this for every question and you'll catch most mistakes before your teacher does. If you're working on a tablet, Chalkmind's Check my work does step 3 for you and explains the rule behind the mistake.
