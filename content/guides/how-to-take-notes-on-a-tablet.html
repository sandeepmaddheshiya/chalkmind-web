A tablet removes the practical problems of paper notes: they don't get lost, you can add space anywhere, and you can write straight onto slides and worksheets. But the tablet doesn't do the learning for you. What turns notes into good marks is the method you use while writing and, even more, what you do with the notes afterwards.

Three principles that matter more than any app

  1. Write it in your own words. Rephrasing forces you to understand. If you can't rephrase something, that's the part to ask about.
  2. Leave room for questions. Notes should prompt you to recall information later, not just store it.
  3. Test, don't reread. Rereading feels productive but fades quickly. Trying to recall the answer before checking it is what builds memory.

Method 1: the Cornell method (best all-rounder)

Split each page into three areas:

  • Main notes (the large right-hand column): what you write during the lesson.
  • Cue column (narrow, on the left): after the lesson, write questions or keywords that the notes answer.
  • Summary (bottom of the page): two or three sentences in your own words.

To revise, cover the main notes and answer the cue questions out loud. On a tablet, draw the lines once on a page, then duplicate the layout, or use ruled paper and a vertical line.

Example: a biology lesson

Cue: “Where does photosynthesis happen?” · Note: “In chloroplasts, mainly in leaf cells; chlorophyll absorbs light.” · Summary: “Plants turn light, water and CO₂ into glucose and oxygen in their chloroplasts.”

Method 2: outlining (best for structured lectures)

Use headings and indented points: main topic, then sub-points, then details. It works well when the teacher follows a clear structure, and it's quick to scan later. On a tablet, leave a gap after each main point so you can add examples or questions afterwards.

Method 3: mapping (best for connected ideas)

Put the topic in the middle and branch out to ideas, causes, effects and examples. Mind maps are good for subjects where ideas connect in many directions, like history, biology and literature, and for summarising a whole chapter on one page. An endless canvas is ideal for this. If you'd like a starting point, an app can draw a first-draft mind map that you then rearrange and add to.

Notes for maths and science

  • Use grid paper so equations and diagrams stay aligned.
  • Write worked examples in full, one step per line, with a short note on why each step happens.
  • Keep a “mistakes I make” page per topic. It becomes your most valuable revision sheet.
  • Draw diagrams large and label everything. Rough shapes are fine; neat labels matter.
  • Check worked examples you copy, especially from the board, since one copied error can confuse you for weeks.

Turning notes into revision

This is the step most students skip, and the one that makes the biggest difference.

  1. Same day (5 minutes): fill in the cue column or write questions in the margin; fix anything unclear while you still remember the lesson.
  2. A few days later (10 minutes): cover your notes and answer your own questions. Mark the ones you got wrong.
  3. Weekly: make flashcards or a short quiz from the marked parts and test yourself again.
  4. Before the test: do practice questions, not rereading. Use your “mistakes I make” pages.

Spacing reviews out like this, and testing yourself each time, works far better than one long session the night before. Apps can help by generating questions from your notes, but answering them from memory is the part that does the work.

Common note-taking mistakes

  • Transcribing everything. You become a typist instead of a thinker.
  • Making notes beautiful. Neat is good; an hour of lettering isn't learning.
  • Never looking at them again. Notes that aren't reviewed are mostly forgotten within weeks.
  • One giant note per subject. Split by lesson or topic, with dates and headings.
  • Copying the board without understanding. Mark anything you don't follow with a “?” and ask about it.
