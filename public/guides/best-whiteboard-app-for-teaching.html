The best whiteboard app for teaching in 2026: how to choose

There's no single best whiteboard app; there's the best one for how you teach. This guide explains what to look for, compares the main options honestly (including the ones that have shut down), and matches them to real teaching situations.

“Best whiteboard app” searches usually come from one of four teachers: someone teaching from the front of a classroom on a projector, someone tutoring or teaching online, someone running group activities where students need to draw too, or someone who just wants a reliable place to write. Those needs pull in different directions, which is why the same app can be perfect for one teacher and frustrating for another.

First: what happened to Jamboard and Microsoft Whiteboard?

Two of the most widely used free whiteboards are no longer options for most teachers:

  • Google Jamboard was discontinued. Google announced the shutdown in 2023, the app became view-only during 2024, and Jam files were no longer available after December 31, 2024.
  • Microsoft Whiteboard has been cut back. Personal Microsoft accounts lost the ability to create and edit whiteboards in September 2026, and support for the standalone apps on Windows, iOS and Android ended from October 16, 2026. Whiteboard continues inside Microsoft Teams and on the web for work and school accounts.

If you relied on either, you're probably here looking for a replacement. The rest of this guide should help. (We also have a dedicated Microsoft Whiteboard alternative page.)

What actually matters when you teach with a whiteboard app

Feature lists are long; these are the things that make a difference in front of a class:

  1. Ink that keeps up. Strokes should appear instantly and look like your handwriting. Lag or wobbly lines are the fastest way to lose a class.
  2. Palm rejection. On a tablet you'll rest your hand on the screen. If that leaves marks, you'll spend the lesson erasing.
  3. Pages or an endless canvas. Pages are easier to navigate and export; an endless canvas suits brainstorming. The best apps let you choose.
  4. Readable on a big screen. Thick pens, a dark background option and easy zoom matter more than 40 brush types.
  5. PDFs and images. Teaching from worksheets, past papers and textbook pages is daily work, so you need to write directly on them.
  6. Export and sharing. Saving a lesson as a PDF for absent students is one of the biggest benefits over a physical board.
  7. Subject tools. For maths: graphs, neat shapes, and ideally help solving or checking problems. For other subjects: diagrams, timelines, mind maps.
  8. Collaboration, if you need it. Students drawing on a shared board is great for group work but adds accounts, links and moderation. Many teachers don't need it.
  9. Privacy and accounts. For school use, check what's stored where and whether students need accounts.
  10. Platform. The app has to run well on the device you'll actually teach from.

The main options, honestly

Chalkmind (Android): best for teaching maths and science from a tablet

What it is: our whiteboard app for Android tablets and phones, built around handwriting. Write a problem and end it with “=”, and it writes the answer in your handwriting with the steps; select any part of the board to explain it, give a hint or check a student's working; import PDFs and highlight words to see their meaning; generate editable mind maps and flowcharts, quizzes and worksheets.

Good for: maths and science teachers and tutors teaching from the front or on a call; students working alone. Limits: Android only; no real-time collaboration (students can't draw on your board); AI features need a free sign-in and have a daily limit unless you subscribe to Plus. More for teachers.

Explain Everything: best for recorded lessons and lesson videos

What it is: a long-standing education whiteboard on tablets and the web, known for recording your board, voice and pointer into a video you can edit on a timeline. It also supports collaboration.

Good for: flipped classrooms and teachers who make explainer videos. Watch out for: it uses a freemium model with paid plans; check the current free-tier limits and Android version reviews before standardising on it.

Samsung Notes: best free option on a Galaxy tablet

What it is: Samsung's note app, preinstalled on Galaxy devices and made for the S Pen. It imports PDFs to write on, converts handwriting to text, and has an endless-canvas mode that works as a simple whiteboard.

Good for: plain writing and PDF annotation if you already own a Galaxy tablet. Limits: it's a notes app rather than a teaching tool: no maths solving, no lesson generation, and it's only on Samsung devices.

Miro: best for collaborative workshops and group activities

What it is: a collaborative online whiteboard with sticky notes, templates and AI features, used widely in business and higher education.

Good for: group brainstorming, project work and activities where every student contributes to the same board. Limits: designed around sticky notes and diagrams rather than handwritten maths; students need access to the board; the free plan is limited, so check Miro’s education plans before rolling it out to a class.

Microsoft OneNote: best for organised class notebooks

What it is: a digital notebook with sections and pages, handwriting, and (on Windows and iPad) a Math Assistant that can solve handwritten equations.

Good for: schools on Microsoft 365 that want lesson materials organised by class. Limits: it's a notebook more than a board, and handwriting features are more limited on Android than on Windows.

Excalidraw: best free tool for quick diagrams

What it is: a free, browser-based whiteboard with a hand-drawn style, good for diagrams, with shareable live-collaboration links.

Good for: diagrams and flowcharts on a laptop, quick sketches during online lessons. Limits: built for mouse and keyboard diagrams more than flowing handwriting; no subject-specific help.

Don't forget the hardware

The app matters less than you think if the hardware fights you. A 10-inch or larger tablet, a stylus with palm rejection, and a reliable way to get the screen onto a projector or into a video call (an HDMI adapter, casting, or screen sharing) will improve any app. Our guide on teaching maths with a tablet covers the setup step by step.

How to try before you commit

  1. Pick two candidates that match your situation from the table above.
  2. Teach the same short lesson in each, ideally on the projector or a test call.
  3. Check the back-row test: can students read your handwriting?
  4. Export the lesson as a PDF and open it on a phone, as a student would.
  5. Check what happens to your data and whether students need accounts.

Questions

What is the best free whiteboard app for teachers?

It depends on your device and how you teach. On a Samsung Galaxy tablet, Samsung Notes is free and good for plain writing. Excalidraw is free in a browser and good for diagrams. Chalkmind's whiteboard is free on Android, with AI help limited to a daily number of answers unless you upgrade.

Is Google Jamboard still available?

No. Google discontinued Jamboard: the app became view-only in 2024 and Jam files were no longer available after December 31, 2024.

Is Microsoft Whiteboard still available?

Only in a reduced form. Microsoft ended support for the standalone Whiteboard apps on Windows, iOS and Android from October 16, 2026; whiteboards continue in Microsoft Teams and on the web for work and school accounts. Personal accounts lost the ability to create and edit boards in September 2026.

Do I need a stylus to teach with a whiteboard app?

For maths and science, yes in practice. Writing equations and diagrams with a finger is slow and hard to read; a stylus with palm rejection makes a tablet feel like a real board.

Which whiteboard app is best for teaching maths?

Look for fast, readable handwriting, a graphing or equation tool, and ideally help with solving or checking work. Chalkmind is built specifically for this; general whiteboards like Miro or Excalidraw are stronger for diagrams and group activities.
